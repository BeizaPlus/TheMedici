Case Summary

Differential: Cystitis, urethritis, pyelonephritis, prostatitis.

This 19-year-old patient is presenting with dysuria with fevers and chills. The differential mentioned above should come to mind. The patient is sexually active, so sexually transmitted infections should be considered as a
cause of his presenting symptoms. The first step is to get an UA, a gram stain, and a urine culture. No blood culture is necessary because the patient does not meet criteria for SIRS. Presence of typical symptoms such as
'edema and tendermess of the prostate on physical exam is enough to confirm the diagnosis. In normal practice, care should be used to not do a vigorous prostate massage because this can increase the risk for bacteremia.
Delaying antimicrobial therapy can increase the risk of complications of bacteremia and abscesses. Fluoroquinolones are the treatment of choice because it can penetrate inflamed prostate. Ciprofloxacin x 14-28 days would
be a good choice. However, Trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole can also be used. In this case, the offending agent is E-coli. E-coli accounts for about a 1/3 of the infections for prostatitis. Other bacteria include: enterococci,
pseudomonas, and S. epidermidis.

Chlamydia and gonorrhea tests should be performed to rule out a sexually transmitted infection,
If the patient had CVA tenderness (suggesting pyelonephritis), then you would use a fluoroquinolone, amoxicillin/clavulanic acid, or an oral cephalosporin as an outpatient. As an inpatient, ceftriaxone IV can be used. In
patients with pyelonephritis, if being adequately treated by antibiotics for 72 hours and still not improving, a CT scan of the abdomen should be done to rule out a renal abscess.

If this weren't the first urinary infection in this patient, an urologic workup should be complete which includes a renal ultrasound, a CT of the abdomen, and a voiding cystography.
