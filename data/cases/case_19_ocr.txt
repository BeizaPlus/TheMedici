Case Summary

The classic triad of diverticulitis is left lower quadrant pain, low-grade fever, and leukocytosis. Patients will experience changes in bowel habits, nausea and vomiting, At times, patients will get dysuria, urinary frequency, and
urgency due to bladder irritation caused by the nearby infected colon. CT scan of the abdomen and pelvis with oral and IV contrast is the gold standard for definitive diagnosis of diverticulitis. Findings of uncomplicated
diverticulitis on CT scan include: bowel wall thickening, pericolic fat stranding, and the presence of colonic diverticula,

Findings of complicated diverticulitis on CT scan (such as this patient) include: formation of abscess, fistula, bowel obstruction, phlegmon, or frank perforation. This patient has a 2 cm abscess. Patients with abscesses <3-4 cm
can be treated with conservative management with antibiotics alone, and those with abscesses >4 cm benefit from percutaneous drainage with referral for surgical follow-up.

Colonoscopy and barium enema are contraindicated in patients with acute diverticulitis due to risk of perforation of an inflamed bowel. Typically, after the diverticulitis resolves, it is important to get a colonoscopy to ensure
there is no underlying colorectal malignancy. However, the patient recently had a colonoscopy which showed no malignancy, so this is not necessary.

Treatment depends on the extent of the disease. It can be as simple as taking oral antibiotics, or in more severe cases, surgical resection. The patient in this case has complicated diverticulitis. Complicated diverticulitis should
be treated with IV cefazolin + Metronidazole, cefuroxime + Metronidazole, or ceftriaxone + Metronidazole or ampicillin/sulbactam alone can be used.

If the patient presented with uncomplicated diverticulitis, oral antibiotics are first line as an outpatient. Metronidazole, fluoroquinolones, or trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole may be used. A high fiber diet is often
recommended as it has been associated with decreased development of diverticulosis.

Diverticulitis complicates 10-25% of diverticulosis cases. Food matter or fecal matter, or fecalith, trapped in the diverticulum abrades the mucosal lining and creates inflammation. It can become impacted and compromise the
penetrating vessels, producing ischemia and microscopic perforation.

If the patient were to have a second or subsequent episode of acute diverticulitis, surgery would need to be consulted to evaluate for resection of the involved segment once the inflammation resolves.

It is important to get an abdominal radiograph to rule out perforation (which would be indicated by free air).
