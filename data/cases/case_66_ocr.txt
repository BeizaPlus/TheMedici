Case Summary

Differential: Fungal lung infection, Lung cancer, Lymphoma, Sarcoidosis, Vasculitis, Lung abscess.

This patient has pulmonary tuberculosis, likely a reactivation of a latent infection based on the location of cavitary lesions in the upper lobes. Suspicion for TB should be high due to the patient being from an endemic area
(Mexico) and having the classic triad of symptoms (fevers, night sweats, and cough). Initial workup includes basic labs and plain chest radiography, which will show upper lobe infiltrates. A CT scan will further demonstrate
cavitary lesions and hilar adenopathy, findings almost pathognomonic for pulmonary tuberculosis. Sputum AFB smear will essentially clinch the diagnosis, as it returns positive for acid-fast bacilli. Mycobacterium cultures
should be collected for drug sensitivity purposes, although these take several weeks to grow. In the meantime, the patient should be hospitalized, placed on respiratory airborne precautions, and started on the empiric 4-drug
regimen for TB: isoniazid, rifampin, pyrazinamide, and ethambutol. It is important to obtain serum chemistries, liver function tests and hepatitis serologies (these were obtained anyway as part of STD screening) as well as a
baseline ophthalmologic exam prior to starting the medications, as isoniazid is hepatotoxic and ethambutol can cause ophthalmologic side effects. Vitamin B6 is also co-administered with isoniazid to prevent neuropathy. The
patient will begin to feel better after 3 days, at which time 3 negative sputum AFB smears should be negative before he is discharged. The patient should be counseled about the importance of medication adherence and
avoiding alcohol and other hepatotoxic substances. Finally, the case should be reported to the local health department so that appropriate contact tracing and testing can be performed.
