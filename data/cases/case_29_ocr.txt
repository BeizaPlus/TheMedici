Case Summary

Differential: Spontaneous abortion, Polyps, Adenomyosis, Fibroids, Bleeding disorders, Gynecological cancers, Urinary tract infection, Polycystic ovarian syndrome, Primary ovarian insufficiency.

The patient presents with abnormal uterine bleeding, which is defined as bleeding outside the normal pattern of a woman's menstrual cycle. Often, the cause of menstrual abnormalities are hormonal imbalances - this
includes disorders such as hyperprolactinemia (pituitary dysregulation, prolactinoma), thyroid dysfunction, and polycystic ovarian syndrome. Bleeding disorders may present with abnormal uterine bleeding, so this may be
ruled out with coagulation studies.

Itis also important to evaluate other causes/locations of bleeding, |.E. the rectum, the vagina, urethra, and cervix. A pelvic ultrasound can determine whether structural pathologies are the cause of bleeding — such as polyps,

fibroids, and cancerous growths.

When all other causes of abnormal uterine bleeding causes are ruled out, it can be assumed that anovulation is the cause. Anovulation is the most common cause of abnormal uterine bleeding in women of childbearing age.
The ovaries produce estrogen but do not undergo ovulation, Therefore, no corpus luteum and progesterone is not released . Continual estrogen stimulation of the endometrium results in increased growth of the blood

supply and sloughing at irregular times,

Hormone therapy (IUDs, oral contraceptives, etc) are given to regulate the patient's menstrual cycle. In this case, the patient can just get high dose oral estrogens. Only estrogen therapy would be the fastest to stop the
bleeding. This should be no more than 21-25 days. Combined progestin/estrogen therapy can be used as well, but this may take longer to stop the bleeding.

This therapy should be avoided in women with absolute contraindications to estrogen therapy (women with DVT, history of MI, history of cerebrovascular accident, history of malignancy, or inherited thrombophilia such as
Factor V leiden or prothrombin gene mutation). Women with the following risk factors can be safely treated with estrogen for short periods (less than 4 weeks): Hypertension, Diabetes, SLE, smoking.

In this case, the patient has a thickened endometrial lining. High dose progestins could also be used as this will inhibit further endometrial growth and organize and support the estrogen-primed endometrium which will
allow effective sloughing upon hormone withdrawal. If the patient does not have a thickened endometrium, progestins are unlikely to be effective.

In summary, estrogen only therapy is faster. Combined estrogen/progestin can be used, but it may take longer. Progestin only is only effective if a patient has anovulatory bleeding with a thickened endometrium.
If you get a patient who has contraindications to estrogen therapy, then you can use progestins or surgery. You can also use tranexamic acid but progestins and surgery would be preferred.

We have received questions about why an endometrial biopsy is considered invasive. The patient is 33 years old and has no risk factors for malignancy (age > 50, taking Tamoxifen, PCOS, obesity, diabetes). The patient does

not require a biopsy. The cause of the thickened endometrial lining is due to estrogen stimulation without progesterone being released due to anovulation.
