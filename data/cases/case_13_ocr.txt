Case Summary

Differential: Acute gastritis, peptic ulcer disease, H. Pylori infection, B-cell lymphoma, gastric cancer, cholelithiasis, cholecystitis, viral gastroenteritis.

The HPI demonstrates patient's extensive use of ibuprofen for his chronic osteoarthritis. Suspicion is therefore high for patient developing NSAID-induced gastropathy. NSAIDs block both COX-1 and COX-2, impairing
production of gastric prostaglandins which maintains healthy mucosa. The condition should first be treated conservatively before other more invasive tests, such as an EGD (esophagogastroduodenoscopy), should be
considered. If the patient does not improve with conservative treatment in the next 2-4 weeks, an EGD would be indicated. An EGD was not pursued since the patient improved with the removal of NSAIDs and the
administration of a prescription of a proton-pump inhibitor. Antibiotics were not given as patient was negative for H. Pylori. Other risk factors could be targeted as nonpharmaceutical therapy, such as smoking and alcohol
use. In addition to Tylenol, topical pharmaceutical NSAIDs (diclofenac gel) are first-line for chronic osteoarthritis. The patient could try this as an alternative to oral NSAIDs for his knee pain.

To be more specific about the question about why points would be taken away for doing an endoscopy: This is excellent example of a case that is frequently seen on the USMLE. The case is there to see if you will restrain from
ordering invasive tests. The important piece of the case is in the history that the patient has been using excessive amounts of Ibuprofen. His symptoms have only been going on for a week. Conservative management is key to
have the patient stop NSAIDs and give a PPI/H2 and have the patient follow-up. If the patient continues to worsen, then you order invasive tests. The patient is medically stable. The patient does have risk factors (two being
his smoking status and his age), but there is no rush to do invasive tests.
