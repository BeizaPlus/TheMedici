This patient most likely has Alzheimer’s dementia, a neurodegenerative disorder of unknown cause, and the most common cause of dementia. Key elements of this patient's history are the decline in memory (getting lost on
her way home) and executive functioning (difficulty paying bills), and her physical exam shows no focal neurologic deficits suggestive of alternative diagnoses that can cause neurocognitive dysfunction (such as chronic
subdural hematoma or normal pressure hydrocephalus). Mental status and neurocognitive assessment shows significant impairment in delayed recall of objects. Lab workup for reversible causes of dementia is unremarkable.
Although neuroimaging does not aid in the diagnosis of Alzheimer's disease, it can rule out underlying structural brain lesions (MRI being more sensitive than CT), but in this patient's case the only finding is generalized brain
atrophy, which is usually age-related and fairly nonspecific. Treatment options for Alzheimer's dementia include cholinesterase inhibitors such a donepezil (most widely used, preferred first-line agent due to its once-daily
dosing and ease of use). Memantine is another treatment option, although it is usually only given in moderate to severe cases, as it has been shown to have little clinical benefit for mild cases. It's important to note that the
cholinesterase inhibitors are only for symptomatic improvement; they do not alter the disease course or slow progression of the disease. Memantine may be neuroprotective, although existing data is mixed on its effects on
disease progression. As always, it's important to give routine vaccinations and recommended screening tests for this patient.
