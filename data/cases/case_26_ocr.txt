Case Summary

Differential: Cystic fibrosis, Failure to thrive, Asthma, Malabsorption syndrome, Sinusitis, Bronchiectasis

Cystic Fibrosis is an autosomal recessive condition due to a mutation in a chloride channel (gene CFTR, located on chromosome 7). It is most common in the Caucasian population. The majority of these cases are detected
through newborn screening in the United States, but individuals that were not born through standardized healthcare systems often remain undiagnosed. A positive newborn screening is indicated by an elevated sweat
chloride level of greater than 60 mEq/L. Abnormal sweat chloride testing is sufficient to diagnose cystic fibrosis in infants as well as non-infants.

The CFTR channel regulates chloride movement in a number of epithelial cells, including the respiratory tract, the pancreas, sweat/salivary glands, intestines, and the reproductive system. This results in thick secretions that
are difficult to clear, and are therefore more prone to developing infections. The most common infectious pathogens include H. influenzae, Staph. Aureus, Pseudomonas Aeruginosa, and Bulkholderia cepacia. Common
findings in patients with cystic fibrosis on physical exam include wheezing, increased anterior/posterior diameter of the chest, digital clubbing, and failure to thrive/difficulty in gaining weight. There are a number of
complications that are associated with cystic fibrosis, but respiratory complication is the number one contributor to morbidity/mortality. Chest physiotherapy and additional antibiotics may improve the condition, but a lung
transplant may be necessary in candidates with severe disease.

The treatment in this case is to stabilize the patient which is done by giving supplemental oxygen and IV fluids. Next, the patient is given enzyme replacement therapy, vitamins, lV antibiotics, and chest physical therapy.
