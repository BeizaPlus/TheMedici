Case Summary
Differential: Domestic abuse, neurological abnormalities causing balance issues, substance abuse, Accident Prone.
A Colles’ fracture is defined a distal radial fracture that is usually dorsally displaced. The most common mechanism of injury is when a patient falls on his/her outstretched hand when in dorsiflexion. Physical exam will present

with tenderness on palpation of the distal forearm on the lateral side. In the case of this patient, if the fracture remains stable, closed reduction of the dorsally displaced radius fracture followed by casting/splinting is essential

for the patient's fracture to heal properly. Pain medications should also be administered.
Complications of a Colles’ fracture include neurovascular complications (IE - median nerve injury, vascular ischemia, compartment syndrome), as well as malunion, co-occurring scaphoid fractures, and ligamentous injuries.

An important take-away from this case is how to handle a patient with domestic abuse. Physicians should reassure the patient that they are available for support. The patient should be connected with social work so that they
may be offered options for counseling and safe havens. It is important to continually offer ongoing support and concern when patients come into the physician's office in order to foster an open line of communication, as the

patient may be reluctant at first to disclose their situation.

Although the patient denies that she is being abused (which is common due to fear of retaliation by the abuser), there is enough evidence to suspect this. The patient has bruises on cheeks, forehead, fractured right ribs
which all are not likely a result from a fall down some stairs. After doing a skeletal survey, the patient is noted to have old fractures of her left ribs. Thus, the patient is either accident prone or more likely is being abused at
home. Substance abuse is also on the differential and a simple urine toxicology screen can rule this out (although the patient may have been sober for many days prior to coming to the doctor which would result in a
negative urine toxicology screen). No tract marks were seen or other evidences that would infer that the patient had problems with substance abuse.
