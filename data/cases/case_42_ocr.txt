Case Summary

Differential: Gout, Bacterial arthritis, rheumatoid arthritis, lyme disease, pseudogout, psoriatic arthritis.

This patient has an acute gout attack. To definitively diagnose the patient, it is important to get an arthrocentesis and analyze the synovial fluid to look for crystals and rule out infectious causes that could cause monoarticular
pain. The patient has needle-shaped, negatively birefringent urate crystals in the synovial fluid which confirms the diagnosis of gout. If the crystals were calcium pyrophosphate crystals (which are weakly positively
birefringent, rod-shaped and rhomboidal shaped crystals), then the patient would have pseudogout. The patient has a fever, so infectious etiology is on the differential. Doing a urethral culture/gram stain, lyme serology are
all indicated to rule out other causes. NSAIDs are the treatment of choice for the patient. Corticosteroids or colchicine are also acceptable. The patient's chlorthalidone should be discontinued because thiazide diuretics
increase serum uric acid levels and can precipitate a gout attack (although when tested during an acute flare may be normal or even low). The patient also states that he drank a lot of alcohol recently which also could
precipitate a gout attack. The patient should be switched to another antihypertensive agent such as an ACE inhibitor like Lisinopril or a calcium channel blocker, like amlodipine. The patient should be counseled to limit his
alcohol intake, limit his purine intake (such as meats), and to hydrate appropriately. In a followup visit in 1-2 weeks, the patient could be put on a prophylactic regimen of allopurinol since the patient's 24-hour urine uric acid
level is above 800mg. However, since this is the first attack and there were multiple factors that could have caused the high uric acid (beer, thiazide diuretic), it would also be reasonable to wait to see if the patient has another
attack before starting him on a prophylactic medication. A discussion with the patient would be warranted,
