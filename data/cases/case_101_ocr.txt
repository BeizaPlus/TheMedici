Case Summary

Differential: Angioedema, Laryngotracheobronchitis (Croup), Caustic ingestion, Foreign body aspiration, Diphtheria, Laryngitis, Peritonsillar abscess, Retropharyngeal abscess.

This patient has epiglottitis, which is typically due to a Haemophilus influenzae type b infection. This is a medical emergency. In epiglottitis, rapid swelling of the epiglottis and surrounding tissues can lead to airway
compromise. Classic features of epiglottitis include difficulty breathing, drooling, and dysphagia. Increased stridor and “tripod” positioning, leaning forward with the neck hyperextended” are both signs of worsening airway
obstruction. This can lead to complete closure of the airway. Typically, the patient is transferred to an operating room while the patient is being intubated; however, this is not an option as there is no “operating room”

location on the test. This patient should be admitted to the ICU where he can be intubated and placed on a respirator if he decompensates. While the incidence of epiglottitis is low, rates are increasing due to the increased

prevalence of non-immunized children.

Diagnosis is confirmed when the epiglottis is observed during laryngoscopy for intubation. Imaging is not necessary to make the diagnosis. Once the airway is secured, blood and throat cultures can be collected. This patient
should be started on a 3rd generation cephalosporin (eg, ceftriaxone) and vancomycin to cover for the most common causes of epiglottitis: Haemophilus influenzae type b, Streptococcus pneumoniae (including strains that
may be penicillin-resistant), Group A Streptococcus, and Staphylococcus aureus (including community-acquired methicillin-resistant S. aureus (MRSA) strains). Because of this bacterial differential, empiric antibiotics should
also include an anti-staphylococcal agent such as vancomycin.
