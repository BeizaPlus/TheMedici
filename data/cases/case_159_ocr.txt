Case Summary

Differential: Major depressive disorder with psychotic features, Stimulant intoxication, Schizophrenia, Borderline personality disorder.
The patient has newly onset bipolar disorder. As the patient had a full manic episode, the patient will be classified as Bipolar |. A single manic episode is enough to qualify for Bipolar | as long as it was not caused by
substances, medications, or any other external factors. To make a diagnosis of a manic episode, you can use the DIG FAST mnemonic. D = Distractibility, | = Indiscretion (increased risky behaviors), G = Grandiosity, F= Flight of

Ideas, A = Increased Activity, S = Sleep deficit, T = Talkativeness.

Three of the DIGFAST symptoms need to be met to have a diagnosis of mania if the patient has euphoric mood (4 if the patient is irritable). The patient has been involved in risky-behavior, talking fast; has increased goal
directed activity such as creating business plans; grandiosity, thinking she is creating companies that will revolutionize the world; and has a decreased need for sleep.

The treatment for a first-time manic episode in a newly onset bipolar patient is a mood stabilizer (mainly lithium or valproate) along with an antipsychotic. Lithium is likely to better choice in this patient as valproate can be
problematic for patients of childbearing age. However, itis still technically correct to use valproate in an acutely manic patient, so credit will be given.

The antipsychotic could be any first- or second-generation antipsychotic. Common antipsychotics used is haloperidol, aripiprazole, olanzapine, quetiapine, or risperidone. Psychiatry should be consulted. Perhaps in the future,
the patient can be maintained on monotherapy of either a mood stabilizer or an antipsychotic, but during this acute manic period, the patient should be started on both.

After a couple days, the patient's mania will subside, and the case will end. The patient should be counseled about safe sex practices, medication compliance, and against heavy alcohol use.
