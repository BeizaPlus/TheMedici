Case Summary

Differential: Androgen-secreting tumor, Thyroid disease, Cushing's syndrome, Idiopathic hirsutism, Nonclassic congenital adrenal hyperplasia, Ovarian neoplasm, Hyperprolactinemia.

This patient has polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS). Clinical suspicion for this diagnosis should be high based on the following: obesity, irregular menstrual cycles, features of hyperandrogenism (hirsutism, acne) and insulin
resistance (hyperpigmented skin in the axillae, also known as acanthosis nigricans). Based on the Rotterdam criteria (see below), she can be diagnosed with PCOS just based on her history and physical alone (oligomenorrhea
+ clinical signs of hyperandrogenism) although a pelvic ultrasound would demonstrate polycystic ovaries. Additional lab testing for workup of oligo- or amenorthea include a urine pregnancy test (beta-hCG), TSH, FSH, and
prolactin. A total testosterone level will also be elevated in this case. Because of the association between obesity, PCOS, and cardiovascular/metabolic disease, itis also appropriate to evaluate the patient for diabetes (fasting
or oral glucose tolerance test or hemoglobin A1c) and dyslipidemia (lipid panel). First-line treatment for PCOS in a patient not pursuing pregnancy is a combined estrogen-progestin oral contraceptive. This typically improves
both the clinical symptoms of hyperandrogenism and menstrual irregularities. An additional pharmacologic option to manage hyperandrogenism is an anti-androgen, such as spironolactone, but this is usually added after the
patient has already been started on a combined oral contraceptive.

Rotterdam criteria: 2 out of 3 of the following criteria are required to make the diagnosis:
Oligo- and/or anovulation

Clinical and/or biochemical signs of hyperandrogenism

Polycystic ovaries (by ultrasound)
