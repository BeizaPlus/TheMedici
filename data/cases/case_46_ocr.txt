Case Summary

Differential: Guillain-Barre Syndrome, Myasthenia gravis, Paraneoplastic neuropathy, HIV peripheral neuropathy, Conversion disorder, Chronic inflammatory demyelinating polyneuropathy, Acute myelopathy (from
compression, transverse myelitis, vascular injury), Toxic neuropathies, West Nile encephalitis.

The patient has Guillain-Barre Syndrome, an immune-mediated polyneuropathy usually triggered by a preceding infection. The most notorious infection is Campylobacter jejuni, bacteria which can cause an acute diarrheal
illness. C. jejuni has proteins that mimic the myelin surrounding nerves, so when the body produces antibodies against these proteins to fight the bacterial infection, they can also attack the myelin around nerves, causing a
demyelinating syndrome (also known as “molecular mimicry’). The demyelination causes progressive, typically symmetric ascending muscle weakness. Given the patient's reported difficulty breathing (despite stable vital signs
and pulse oximetry) this is a red flag for impending respiratory muscle involvement and possible respiratory failure, so he should be urgently admitted for further workup and management. The patient should get lab work
done, including a lumbar puncture and CSF analyses, to rule out other causes of muscle weakness and polyneuropathy. Electrodiagnostic studies (EMG, nerve conduction studies), in addition to the clinical history and exam,
will help make the diagnosis of GBS. Spinal MRI may be helpful but is not actually needed to make a diagnosis. Once a diagnosis of GBS is made, neurology should be consulted and he should start on IVIG or plasma
exchange as soon as possible to prevent respiratory failure and the need for mechanical ventilation. If the patient is not treated within an appropriate time, the patient will stop breathing due to respiratory muscle weakness.
‘Once the patient gets treatment for 3-7 days, the patient improves and can be discharged with followup.
