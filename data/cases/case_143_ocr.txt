Case Summary

Differential: Glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase deficiency, Biliary atresia

The patient has the signs of classic galactosemia which include: jaundice, vomiting, hepatomegaly, poor feeding, lethargy, and cataracts. Other findings commonly seen that is not present in this case are: sepsis, diarrhea, and
acute liver failure. The cause of classic galactosemia is a deficiency of the galactose-1-phosphate uridyl transferase (GALT) enzyme.

With classic galactosemia, infants are typically born without complications and appear healthy. Most infants have neonatal screenings done, but these can take 7-30 days to return, After 1-2 days, the patient will begin to
show symptoms after serum galactose levels increase.

This is a difficult case as this may appear to be a routine breast-feeding neonatal jaundice case. It is tempting to just put the baby under bili-lights and advance the clock. However, there are concerning findings which include
elevated liver enzymes and bilateral cataracts (which would be missed if a HEENT exam is not done). These findings which would differentiate from a breast-feeding neonatal jaundice case.

The gold standard to detect classic galactosemia is to send for DNA sequencing (Sanger sequencing). This is not an order on the USMLE at this time, so clinical information and laboratory data should be used to guide your
diagnosis and treatment.

The treatment is to simply put the patient on a lactose and galactose free diet. The patient will need to stop drinking breast milk and switch to a soy-based infant formula. The patient will need to be admitted to the inpatient

unit and monitored. The patient will improve after the removal of lactose/galactose from the diet.
