This patient has acute pyelonephritis, which by definition is considered a complicated urinary tract infection (a UTI that has extended beyond the bladder). The patient in this case is otherwise healthy and does not have other
conditions that would affect management (such as pregnancy or immunocompromised state). Acute pyelonephritis is suggested by fevers, chills, flank pain, costovertebral angle tendemess, and nausea or vomiting, with or
without the typical symptoms of cystitis. This patient has fevers/chills/flank pain. Outpatient management is acceptable for patients with mild to moderate illness who are not septic or have evidence of obstruction or renal
impairment who can be stabilized with rehydration (if needed) and antibiotics in an outpatient facility and discharged on oral antibiotics under close supervision. Inpatient management is warranted if the patient has sepsis,
evidence of urinary obstruction, severe pain, debility, inability to tolerate oral medications or oral hydration, pregnancy, or if there is concern the patient will be non-compliant with the medication. In this case, the patient can
be managed as an outpatient and inpatient admission is not necessarily warranted. Empiric treatment is with a fluoroquinolone (ciprofloxacin or levofloxacin x 5-7 days) if there are no risk factors for multi-drug resistant
bacteria or contraindications to fluoroquinolone therapy. This patient's urine culture came back as susceptible to fluoroquinolones so she can continue with this regimen. Another acceptable option for outpatient therapy is
trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole. Inpatient parenteral antibiotic options include ceftriaxone, gentamicin, ertapenem, or tobramycin. These broader spectrum IV antibiotics carry many risks, so it's important to start on first-line
