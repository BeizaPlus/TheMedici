potassium deficiency. It is gentle on the gastrointestinal tract, making it more tolerable for some patients. Potassium gluconate is suitable for long-term supplementation and maintenance but is not typically used for
treating acute hypokalemia because it provides less potassium per dose compared to other salts and is less effective for quickly raising serum potassium levels in severe cases. In this case, with a patient with
hypokalemia and possible cirrhosis, giving potassium citrate could has an alkalinizing effect, which can affect acid-base balance and might be problematic in cirrhosis, especially if there is a risk of metabolic alkalosis or

if the patient is experiencing any acid-base disturbances.

Alcohol withdrawal has a wide range of symptoms, that can range from tremulousness to delirium tremens. This patient is presenting with delirium tremens, which is characterized by autonomic instability, hallucinations,

agitation, and altered mental status. Patients that present with seizures or delirium tremens are advised to receive IV therapy with benzodiazepines. It should also be noted that benzodiazepines are metabolized by the liver,
and those with severe liver disease should avoid long-acting benzodiazepines. Lorazepam and oxazepam should be used in this case due to their shorter half-life, preventing the unwanted side effect of over-sedation.

For patients that do not have liver disease, long-acting benzodiazepines would be preferred as they provide a lower chance of recurrent withdrawal or seizures. This includes chlordiazepoxide and diazepam. Refractory
delirium tremens is common in patients with chronic alcohol abuse, as they have developed low endogenous GABA concentrations or resistant GABA receptors.
