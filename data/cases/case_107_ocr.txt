Correctly Avoided
© Clozapine

Reason: Avoid Clozapine. A lot of attention is given to clozapine as it is used for treatment resistant psychotic/mood disorders. However, it is incorrect to give clozapine to a first onset psychotic patient. The patient is
NOT more likely to respond to clozapine more than a first- or second-generation antipsychotic. Clozapine should be used only for treatment resistant cases. The definition of treatment resistant can vary. Some experts
say that an adequate trial and failure of 2 antipsychotics would be defined as treatment resistant. In reality, most patients receive clozapine only as a last resort due to the weekly blood draws, risks of myocarditis,

seizures, potentially fatal neutropenia, metabolic syndrome, and severe constipation. For a first episode psychosis, avoid clozapine.

Case Summary

Differential diagnosis: Substance induced psychotic disorder, Schizoaffective disorder, Psychosis secondary to a medical condition, Major depressive disorder with psychotic features, Bipolar disorder, Delusional disorder,
Schizophreniform disorder.

The patient meets DSM-5 criteria for schizophrenia, The patient has both delusions (thinking aliens possessed his neighbor. This is a bizarre delusion) and hallucinations (hearing voices telling him that his neighbor is
dangerous). These symptoms are obviously impairing his life and has been going on for over 6 months which was obtained through collateral information from his mother. There was no history of a mood disorder such as
mania or depression. The patient did admit to using marijuana which was also positive on his urine drug screen, but the severity of the symptoms and the duration would make Schizophrenia the more appropriate diagnosis.
The patient also is in the standard age group where patients who develop Schizophrenia typically will manifest symptoms (which is in their late teens to early 20s). College is a common time where patients will manifest their
first episode of psychosis as it can be a stressful time away from support systems.

The most common treatments in an inpatient psychiatric setting or an emergency room setting with an uncooperative patient would be to offer oral medication, and if the patient refuses, then you would give IM
antipsychotics. This is typically IM Haldol, IM Olanzapine (Zyprexa), IM Aripiprazole (Abilify), or IM Ziprasidone (Geodon). Haldol is the most commonly administered in hospital emergency settings due to availability. If the
patient is willing to take oral, then oral Olanzapine (Zyprexa Zydis) is very popular because it is a dissolvable wafer that is very fast acting. If Haldol is given, this is typically administered with Lorazepam (Ativan) along with
Cogentin or Benadryl to avoid a potential EPS (Extrapyramidal Syndrome) including dystonias. This can be given while using IM second generation antipsychotics, but not necessary. A typical regimen given is colloquially
called the 5-2-1 meaning 5mg of IM Haldol, 2mg of IM Ativan, and Img of IM Cogentin. As Cogentin IM is very expensive, an alternative is a 5-2-50 which is 5mg of IM Haldol, 2mg of IM Ativan, and 50mg of IM Benadryl
(Diphenhydramine).

It is important to note that it is contraindicated to administer IM benzodiazepines when using IM Olanzapine (Zyprexa) due to the risk for fatal respiratory depression.

There has been a lot of debate on if itis better to start a patient on a first generation or second-generation antipsychotic. The answer is nuanced and above the scope of the test, but essentially, they both are effective in
treating psychosis, but you are choosing side effect profiles. First generation antipsychotics such as Haldol have a higher risk for akathisia, EPS, and tardive dyskinesia while second generation antipsychotics have a higher risk
for metabolic issues such as weight gain, diabetes, and cholesterol problems. In reality, most patients are initially treated emergently with IM Haldol and then transitioned to a second-generation antipsychotic. Tardive
dyskinesia can be a permanent condition and is a favorite source of litigation for lawyers. It is much harder to prove that a patient gained weight, became diabetic, and had cholesterol issues which can be directly tied to the
medication. Schizophrenia patients tend to not have healthy diets due to lack of access to fresh fruits/vegetables due to socioeconomic forces. The argument can be made that their diet has caused their metabolic issues. On
the USMLE, either first generation or second generation will be accepted as an acceptable treatment for schizophrenia. There are quite a few antipsychotics to choose from, and no antipsychotic has been proven to be more

effective than one another (except clozapine but see above as to why this is incorrect to use for a first episode psychotic patient).
In the real world, once the patient has become cooperative, it is important to get a baseline AIMS to monitor the medication in case the patient starts to develop abnormal muscular movements associated with tardive

dyskinesia, The AIMS test is not an order on the USMLE right now(surprise, surprise), but be aware that this is standard of care.
