Case Summary

Differential: Metabolic disease, Neurologic disease, Nonmetal intoxication, Other heavy metal toxicity, Epilepsy, Acute porphyria attack.

This patient has lead intoxication with encephalopathy; this is an emergency requiring prompt diagnosis and immediate chelation therapy! The patient presents with symptoms of lethargy, abdominal pain, vomiting, and
seizures in addition to a classic history of living in an older home undergoing renovation, which should raise your suspicion for lead poisoning. Basic labs (CBC, electrolytes, urinalysis, liver function tests) should be obtained in
addition to blood lead levels (which will show a blood lead level of 80 mcg/dl). The blood lead level in addition to findings of basophilic stippling on the peripheral smear will clinch the diagnosis of lead intoxication. Iron
studies should also be obtained as the patient also has a microcytic anemia; iron deficiency often exists alongside lead poisoning because risk factors for these conditions are similar. Finally, imaging studies including
abdominal x-rays (to look for ingestion of radio-opaque, lead-containing objects) and a head CT (to look for evidence of increased ICP) should be obtained in patients with symptomatic lead toxicity. The patient should be
admitted to the hospital, given IV fluids, lorazepam to control the seizures, and chelation therapy consisting of IV EDTA and IM Dimercaprol. The patient's symptoms will improve and his blood lead levels will fall over the
course of several days, at which point he will be safe to be discharged. It is important to counsel the family about the dangers of lead paint and attempt to involve social work or a lead abatement agency in assessing the
patient's home, as this was the most likely cause of the patient's elevated lead levels. Of course treatment of his acute lead intoxication is life-saving, but unless the home environment changes, he is at risk of future lead
intoxication.
