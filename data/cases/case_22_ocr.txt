Case Summary

Differential: Hypoglycemia, Hyperglycemia, Toxic ingestion, Opioid overdose, Hypothyroidism

Tricyclic antidepressants poisoning often presents with sedation and may include symptoms of delirium or hallucinations. Cardiac arrhythmias is one of the major side effects to look out for. A common mnemonic for TCA

poisoning is the ‘three C's’: Coma, convulsions, and cardiac.

Cardiovascular effects mainly present with widened QRS, prolonged QT, or prolonged PR intervals. It is important to initiate cardiac monitoring and to provide sodium bicarbonate when the patient is presenting with these
EKG changes. Sodium bicarbonate works in two ways: The increase in serum pH favors the non-ionized form of the TCA, so less of the drug binds to sodium channels. The increase in extracellular sodium overcomes the
blockade of sodium channels carried about by TCAs. Lidocaine is another alternative. For those that are hemodynamically unstable, they may require a shock.

TCAs have also been associated with anticholinergic properties, precipitating seizures. Diazepam or phenobarbital are often used to prevent actively seizing patients

Hypotension is also another common presentation in TCA overdose — IV fluids, sodium bicarbonate, and epinephrine/norepinephrine if needed are given to maintain patient's blood pressure status. This is caused by TCA’s
ability to block alpha-1 receptors. Blocking alpha-1 receptors causes sinus tachycardia, cardiac arrhythmias, vasodilatation, hypotension and asystole. This also exerts a number of effects on the central nervous system, leading

to drowsiness coma, respiratory depression and seizures.

The patient is hypoxic with a pulse ox of 75% (ABG also shows that the patient is hypoxic) Oxygen should be started, IV access gained, and patient should be intubated and put on a ventilator. The patient should have a head
CT when stable to rule out any acute injury that could be exacerbating the overdose.

Aspirin and Acetaminophen levels are standard for overdose in case the patient were to take these in conjunction with the TCA which in common in many overdose cases.

Activated charcoal is the most beneficial if the patient took the TCA within 2 hours which is unknown in this patient. It should still be given. Once the patient is awake and can protect her airway after 24 hours, the patient can
be extubated. Psychiatry should be consulted in this case to determine disposition. The patient may require an inpatient stay in the psychiatric inpatient unit. The patient's blood pressure was >70mmHg. If it were lower,
epinephrine would have to be administered or another agent to increase her blood pressure. The patient's EKG resolves with time and with alkalinization.
