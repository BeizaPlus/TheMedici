Case Summary

Differential diagnosis: Primary nocturnal enuresis, secondary enuresis due to obstructive sleep apnea (OSA), diabetes mellitus, urinary tract infection, constipation, neurogenic bladder, seizure disorder, psychosocial stressors.

This patient has severe obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) accompanied by secondary nocturnal enuresis. In children and adolescents, OSA is frequently caused by adenotonsillar hypertrophy, which is often made worse by
obesity. The key clinical features of OSA in this age group include loud snoring every night, witnessed pauses in breathing (apneas), habitual mouth breathing, daytime sleepiness or fatigue, and behavioral problems such as
inattention and irritability that can mimic attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD).

The secondary enuresis occurs because OSA leads to nocturnal polyuria—triggered by negative intrathoracic pressure during obstructed breathing, release of atrial natriuretic peptide, and an elevated arousal threshold that
prevents the child from waking to a full bladder. Obesity further increases the risk by worsening upper airway collapsibility and serving as a major contributing factor.

The initial evaluation should include a urinalysis to rule out urinary tract infection or glucosuria as potential causes of the enuresis. Because prediabetes is common in obese patients with OSA, fasting glucose and/or HbA1c
should be checked. A lipid panel is also warranted to screen for dyslipidemia and features of metabolic syndrome.

The confirmatory diagnostic test is an overnight polysomnography (sleep study), which establishes the diagnosis of OSA and quantifies its severity. In pediatric patients, an apnea-hypopnea index (AHI) greater than 10 events
per hour indicates severe OSA. In this case, the results show an AHI of 25-35 events per hour along with oxygen desaturations.

According to guidelines from the American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) and the American Academy of Otolaryngology-Head and Neck Surgery (AAO-HNS), the first-line treatment for OSA associated with adenotonsillar
hypertrophy is adenotonsillectomy (surgical removal of the tonsils and adenoids), and referral to an otolaryngologist (ENT) is appropriate even in obese adolescents. This procedure typically leads to significant improvement
in the AHI, resolution of enuresis in many cases, and better behavior and quality of life. However, obesity raises the risk of residual OSA after surgery (persistence in approximately 40-60% of obese children), so aggressive
counseling on weight loss through dietary changes and increased physical activity is essential. This approach also helps manage the patient's prediabetes and dyslipidemia while reducing the likelihood of OSA recurrence.
Continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) is considered a second-line option, reserved for cases of persistent OSA after adenotonsillectomy, when surgery is refused or contraindicated, or when adherence to other measures

is poor; however, long-term adherence to CPAP can be difficult in teenagers.

Enuresis-specific treatments, such as desmopressin or an enuresis alarm, should not be started initially. The underlying OSA should be treated first, because the bedwetting often resolves completely or substantially improves
after adenotonsillectomy. Similarly, referral to psychiatry or initiation of medications for presumed ADHD should be deferred until the OSA is addressed, as the behavioral symptoms frequently improve once sleep quality is

restored.
For preventive care, provide intensive lifestyle counseling with a realistic weight loss goal of 0.5~1 kg per week, achieved through a structured calorie deficit and increased daily activity. Prediabetes and dyslipidemia should be
managed primarily with diet and exercise; metformin is rarely used as first-line therapy in pediatric prediabetes unless there is clear progression toward type 2 diabetes. After adenotonsillectomy, schedule follow-up in 4-8

weeks to reassess symptoms, enuresis, and behavior. If symptoms persist, consider repeating polysomnography to evaluate for residual OSA.

‘Common pitfalls to avoid include ordering desmopressin at the outset (which only treats the symptom rather than the underlying cause), initiating CPAP as the primary therapy without first attempting adenotonsillectomy,
failing to screen for metabolic complications (prediabetes and dyslipidemia), or delaying referral to ENT.
