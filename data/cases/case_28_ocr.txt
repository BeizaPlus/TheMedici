Case Summary

Differential: Pelvic inflammatory disease, Acute appendicitis, Dysmenorrhea, Endometriosis, Urinary tract infection, Ectopic pregnancy
This patient has pelvic inflammatory disease. She meets clinical criteria with adnexal tenderness, abdominal tenderness, cervical motion tenderness, fever, and leukocytosis. The patient's cervical culture is positive for N.
gonorrhoeae. A CBC, gram stain, and culture will solidify the diagnosis.

Pelvic inflammatory disease is consequence of untreated sexually-transmitted infections and results in complications such as ectopic pregnancy, Fitz-Hugh Curtis syndrome, and infertility. The pathology results from an
ascending infection from the vagina that travels up the cervix and into the uterus, entering the intraperitoneal cavity through the fallopian tubes.

Gonorrhea and chlamydia are the principal organisms that cause pelvic inflammatory disease, but most cases are thought to be polymicrobial in nature. Risk factors for PID include multiple partners, recent history of
douching, prior history of PID, and cigarette smoking. Clinical symptoms are lower abdominal pain specifically localized to the uterine and adnexal regions, tenderness in the bimanual exam, cervical motion tenderness

(chandelier sign), vaginal bleeding/discharge, nausea/diarrhea, and painful urination.

Outpatient oral treatment of mild to moderate PID are ceftriaxone plus doxycycline, with or without metronidazole. Severe PID is treated with IV cefotetan or cefoxitin, accompanied by oral doxycycline.
Normal labs and vaginal ultrasound rule out other causes of abdominal pain. The patient should be admitted to the hospital (patient is nulliparous and has a fever with abdominal pain) and be given IV antibiotics. Other
criteria for inpatient treatment include elevated WBC, adolescence, peritoneal symptoms, uncertain diagnosis, failure to respond to 48 hours of outpatient management, inability to tolerate outpatient treatment, pregnancy
and immunodeficiency.
