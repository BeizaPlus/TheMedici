Case Summary

Differential: This case is a classic presentation of myasthenia gravis. However, other things that should be in your differential are: Lambert-Eaton myasthenic syndrome, chronic inflammatory demyelinating polyneuropathy,
and motor neuron disease.

The prevalence of Myasthenia Gravis is 1 in 7500. The cause of myasthenia gravis is an autoimmune antibody against the acetylcholine receptor which is causes skeletal muscle weakness with preservation of sensation and
reflexes. The likelihood of having a positive antibody against the acetylcholine receptor is about 80% (50% if it only involves the ocular muscles). Weakness is exacerbated by continued use of the muscle and improved by rest.
The kind of muscles involved can vary but can included extraocular muscles (causing blurry vision), eyelids (causing ptosis), facial muscles (causing difficulty in chewing and slurred speech). Myasthenia Gravis can also cause
limb muscle weakness (proximal and asymmetric). Generally, the classic symptoms are: ptosis, dysarthria, and dysphagia. Electromyography (EMG) can confirm the diagnosis - repetitive stimulation would demonstrate a
decreased signal strength. This can differentiate Myasthenia gravis from Lambert-Eaton, should would show the opposite - an increased neuromuscular signal with repetitive stimulation.

The CT shows a thymoma which occurs in 15% of the patients. It is essential to remove a thymoma as this leads to improvement in symptoms and outcome in about 85% of patients. Treatment would include
anticholinesterase medications such as pyridostigmine. Immunosuppressives are also mainstay treatments as Myasthenia gravis is an autoimmune condition. Prednisone, azathioprine, cyclophosphamide, cyclosporine are all
treatments. If the patient is still not improving, plasmapheresis can be done to remove antibodies against the acetylcholine receptors. If plasmapheresis is ineffective, IVIG can also be used as a treatment.

A question arises as to why you do not just use pytidostigmine as monotherapy. This patient is having respiratory symptoms and has a low oxygen level on presentation. Pyridostigmine does not treat the underlying disease
and only helps with symptoms. Also, pyridostigmine will cause a cholinergic effect and increase airway secretions which may worsen any pulmonary complications. Therefore, you should use pyridostigmine in conjunction
with something that treats the underlying disease such as steroids or azathioprine. Using pyridostigmine as monotherapy might be appropriate for mild cases of myasthenia gravis where the patient has mild symptoms of
ptosis, fatigue, or muscle weakness. However, once the respiratory system becomes involved, it should be treated aggressively and monitored closely to prevent respiratory distress.
