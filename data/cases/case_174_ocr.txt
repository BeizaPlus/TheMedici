Case Summary
Differential: Esophageal varices, peptic ulcer disease, Mallory-Weiss tear, gastric erosions, malignancy.
Esophageal varices are dilated submucosal veins that develop as a consequence of portal hypertension, most 
commonly from cirrhosis. As portal pressure increases, portosystemic collaterals form to decompress the 
portal system, including vessels in the esophagus and gastric fundus. These thin-walled varices are prone to 
rupture, causing life-threatening upper GI bleeding. Patients with cirrhosis often have concurrent 
coagulopathy (elevated INR from decreased clotting factor synthesis) and thrombocytopenia (from 
hypersplenism), which can worsen bleeding. This case presents a classic scenario: a 55-year-old man with 

alcoholic cirrhosis presenting with hematemesis and melena, found to have severe anemia (Hct 24%, Hgb 8.0), 
mild coagulopathy (INR 1.8), and thrombocytopenia (platelets 100,000).
The diagnosis begins with a focused physical examination assessing for signs of shock (tachycardia, 
hypotension), stigmata of chronic liver disease (jaundice, ascites, splenomegaly), and confirming GI bleeding 
(rectal exam for melena). Essential laboratory testing includes CBC (to quantify anemia and 
thrombocytopenia), comprehensive metabolic panel or basic metabolic panel (elevated BUN/Cr ratio suggests 
upper GI bleed), liver function tests (confirm cirrhosis), and coagulation studies (PT/INR to assess synthetic 
function). An abdominal ultrasound can confirm cirrhosis with nodular liver contour, splenomegaly, and 
ascites. The definitive diagnosis is made via urgent esophagogastroduodenoscopy (EGD) with band ligation, 
which is both diagnostic and therapeutic—this is the single most important intervention.
Acute management focuses on careful resuscitation and pharmacologic therapy to control bleeding. Type and 
crossmatch for blood products immediately. Provide IV fluid resuscitation with normal saline, but avoid over-
resuscitation as excessive volume can increase portal pressure and worsen bleeding. PRBC transfusion should 
follow a restrictive strategy, targeting hemoglobin 7-9 g/dL rather than aggressive transfusion, as over-
transfusion paradoxically increases rebleeding risk. With platelets at 100,000 and INR at 1.8, this patient does 
NOT require platelet or FFP transfusions—these would only be indicated if platelets <50,000 or significantly 
higher INR with active bleeding.
Octreotide IV is critical—this vasoactive drug causes splanchnic vasoconstriction, reducing portal pressure and 
bleeding. Alternatives include somatostatin, terlipressin (preferred in Europe), or vasopressin with nitroglycerin 
(rarely used). Antibiotic prophylaxis with ceftriaxone IV is essential in all cirrhotic patients with GI bleeding, as 
it prevents spontaneous bacterial peritonitis and reduces mortality by 10-15%. Ceftriaxone or cefotaxime are 
superior choices; quinolones (norfloxacin, ciprofloxacin) are acceptable but inferior due to rising resistance. PPI 
therapy with pantoprazole IV helps stabilize clots, though it's less critical in variceal bleeding than peptic ulcer 
disease. The patient should be made NPO to prepare for urgent endoscopy.
After acute stabilization, several preventive measures are crucial. Non-selective beta-blockers such as 
propranolol, nadolol, or carvedilol should be started for secondary prophylaxis against rebleeding once the 
patient is hemodynamically stable—these are NOT given during acute bleeding as they can worsen 
hypotension. Beta-blockers reduce portal pressure and significantly decrease rebleeding rates. Hepatitis A and 
B vaccination should be administered to all cirrhotic patients to prevent superinfection that could cause 
further hepatic decompensation. Finally, alcohol cessation counseling is essential to prevent progression of 
cirrhosis and reduce risk of future variceal hemorrhage. These preventive measures are implemented after the 
acute bleed is controlled and should be part of discharge planning.
Two medications commonly used in cirrhosis management warrant specific mention in this context. Lactulose 
should be initiated to prevent hepatic encephalopathy, a frequent and serious complication following upper 
GI bleeding in cirrhotic patients. Digested blood in the gut provides a large protein load, driving intestinal 
ammonia production; lactulose acidifies the colon to trap ammonia as ammonium (NH₄⁺) and accelerates gut 

transit to eliminate it. This is standard preventive care in any cirrhotic patient with significant GI bleeding, even 
in the absence of overt encephalopathy at presentation. Spironolactone, on the other hand, is an aldosterone 
antagonist diuretic commonly used for chronic ascites management in cirrhosis, typically in combination with 
furosemide. However, spironolactone is contraindicated during acute variceal hemorrhage — the patient is 
already volume-depleted and hemodynamically unstable, and administering a diuretic in this setting risks 
worsening hypotension and acute kidney injury. Spironolactone should only be considered after the acute 
bleed has been controlled and the patient is hemodynamically stable, if ascites management is required as 
part of ongoing outpatient cirrhosis care.
