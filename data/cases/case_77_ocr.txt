Case Summary

Differential: Anxiety, Substance Abuse, Hyperthyroidism, Mitral valve prolapse, Panic attack.

This patient has new-onset atrial fibrillation with rapid ventricular response. She is presenting because she is symptomatic, with chest pain, palpitations, and dizziness. The trick with this case is the patient's history of an
anxiety disorder and her recent stressors. However, as with all psychiatric disorders, a panic attack (which may present similarly) should be a diagnosis of exclusion and medical causes must be investigated and ruled out first.
In this case, the diagnosis is made relatively quickly with a 12-lead EKG. In addition to a history and physical exam, workup for new-onset atrial fibrillation in a hemodynamically stable patient includes 12-lead EKG, complete
blood count, serum electrolytes (especially for renal function, as this can preclude the patient from taking certain anticoagulants), tests to evaluate for predisposing causes (ex: TSH, as hyperthyroidism can trigger atrial
fibrillation), and chest x-ray. Echocardiogram should be done to evaluate for structural or valvular heart disease, but this doesn’t necessarily have to be done right away in stable patients, as results would likely not change
initial management decisions. Management in this patient includes rate control with a beta blocker (such as metoprolol) or calcium channel blocker (diltiazem or verapamil) and then initiation of anticoagulation. The decision
to anticoagulate patients is based on the CHADS-VASC score. This patient scores a 3 (1 point for female sex, 1 point for age > 65, 1 point for hypertension history), so guidelines strongly recommend anticoagulation. After
receiving appropriate rate controlling medication, the patient's heart rate will decrease, her symptoms will improve, and she will spontaneously convert back to sinus rhythm (as most patients with new-onset atrial fibrillation
do within 48-72 hours). Cardioversion can be considered in a few weeks, but she will need to have been on appropriate anticoagulation for at least 3 weeks to minimize the risk of stroke periprocedurally.

In terms of choice of anticoagulation, updates to the USMLE now include DOACs (Direct Oral Anti-coagulants) including: apixaban, edoxaban, rivaroxaban, and dabigatran. These were previously unavailable. These are now
acceptable choices for anticoagulation. Coumadin is still considered gold-standard treatment for anticoagulating, At times outpatient Coumadin clinics may not be available, so an inpatient hospitalization may be warranted
to titrate up warfarin to an INR of 2.5, so both outpatient and inpatient locations would be acceptable for this case.

The question has been asked why this patient shouldn't be considered hemodynamically unstable and undergo cardioversion, Hemodynamic instability is typically defined as inadequate perfusion to vital organs. Having
severe tachycardia can lead to hemodynamic instability, but the patient is not there yet as the patient has a normal blood pressure. You typically start with rate control, and if the patient does not respond to rate control, then
you could consider cardioversion.

References regarding this topic:
AHA/ACC (2014, updated 2019): For AFib, hemodynamic instability (hypotension, shock, or acute heart failure due to rapid rate) warrants urgent cardioversion, Stable patients with AFib and rapid HR are managed with rate

control and evaluation of underlying causes (e.g, ischemia).

AHA/ACC Chest Pain Guidelines (2021): Chest pain with possible acute coronary syndrome requires rapid ECG, troponins, and anti-ischemic therapy. AFib complicating ischemia may necessitate rate control or cardioversion if

instability develops.

ESC (2020): Similar to AHA, recommends rate control for stable AFib with RVR and urgent cardioversion for instability.
