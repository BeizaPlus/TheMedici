Case Summary
Differential: Goesophageal reflux disease, peptic ulcer, gastroenteritis, gastric ulcer, nonulcer dyspepsia, biliary colic or cholecystitis, pancreatitis, irritable bowel syndrome.

Peptic ulcer disease occurs when the mucosal wall of the duodenum and stomach are disrupted, resulting in recurrent episodes of abdominal pain, often located in the epigastric region. A number of factors can contribute to
the development of peptic ulcer disease, such as: H. Pylori Infection; Drugs (NSAIDs, SSRI); Alcohol; Smoking; Physiologic stress (IE burns, head trauma, surgery); Acid hypersecretion.

The most common cause of peptic ulcer disease is H. Pylori infection. It disrupts the mucosal lining by increasing acid secretion and inhibiting bicarbonate production. In the case of this patient, he has developed a duodenal.
ulcer secondary to H. Pylori infection. Pain derived from duodenal ulcers are classically improved with food ingestion. Upper endoscopy is the gold standard for evaluating patients with peptic ulcer disease, especially through
mucosal biopsy. Other diagnostic methods include urease breath test, fecal H. Pylori antigen testing, and H. Pylori serum antibody testing. A major complication of peptic ulcer disease is bleeding, which could lead to

hemorrhage and perforation. Triple therapy consists of clarithromycin, amoxicillin, and a proton-pump inhibitor. Quadruple therapy is given to those that may have resistance to clarithromycin — which includes metronidazole,

bismuth, tetracycline, and a proton-pump inhibitor.
