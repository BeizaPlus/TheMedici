Case Summary

Differential: Congestive heart failure, Acute renal failure, Acute myocardial infarction, Acute respiratory distress syndrome, Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, Pneumonia, Pulmonary embolism, Pulmonary edema

This patient is experiencing congestive heart failure with reduced ejection fraction, often referred to as systolic dysfunction heart failure. His heart failure is likely the result of old age and his prior myocardial infarction that
damaged his cardiac tissue. This creates a condition where the heart is unable to contract and pump enough blood to meet his cardiac needs. The most common cause of this form of heart failure is cardiac ischemia and
coronary artery disease. The condition presents with symptoms of fluid overload, such as pulmonary congestion, shortness of breath, paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnea (difficulty breathing), orthopnea (difficulty breathing in
recumbent position; relieved by elevation of the head will pillows), and nocturia. When heart failure becomes severe, the patient could also experience hemoptysis, resulting from increased pressure in the alveolar capillaries.
Macrophages in the alveoli will digest the red blood cells, creating hemosiderin-laden macrophages, also known as heart failure cells.

Diagnosis of congestive heart failure is done through the patient's clinical history and physical exam, and is supported by a number of diagnostic tests. On auscultation, an 3 murmur will be heard on exam, and pulmonary
‘edema will present with rales and crackles and may also be accompanied by expiratory wheezing. Brain natriuretic peptide (BNP) is elevated in these patients, and an echocardiogram will demonstrate reduced ejection
fraction in systolic dysfunction.

The easiest way to think about this is defined by the New York Heart Association (NYHA) Classification of congestive heart failure.

NYHA class |: Symptoms only occur with vigorous activities, such as playing a sport. Patients are nearly asymptomatic;
NYHA class II: Symptoms occur with prolonged or moderate exertion, such as climbing a flight of stairs or carrying heavy packages. Slight limitation of activities;
NYHA class Ill: Symptoms occur with usual activities of daily living, such as walking across the room or getting dressed. Markedly limiting;

NYHA class IV: Symptoms occur at rest;

This classification helps with treatment. In general, this is how you would treat a patient with CHF:

Mild CHF (NYHA Class | to II):

Restrict sodium intake (<4 g sodium) and mildly restrict physical activity;

Start loop diuretic if patient is volume overloaded or pulmonary congestion is present

Use an ACE inhibitor as first-line agent;

Mild to moderate CHF (NYHA Class I! to Ill):

Start a diuretic (loop diuretic) and an ACE inhibitor,

Add a beta-blocker;

Moderate to Severe CHF (NYHA class Ill to IV):

Add digoxin to loop diuretic and ACE inhibitor and beta blocker;

Add spironolactone

The patient has moderate to Severe CHF, so the patient should be started on a loop diuretic to diurese excess fluids, continue his ACE inhibitor (patient already on lisinopril), and give a beta blockers. Carvedilol is the beta-

blocker of choice. In the COMET trial, carvedilol led to significant improvement in survival compared with metoprolol.

The patient might be able to be managed as an outpatient, but since this is the first time that the patient has had symptoms, the patient would benefit from inpatient admission so that the workup could be complete more
quickly (echocardiogram to assess ejection fraction) as an inpatient. The patient would receive IV diuretics which work more quickly. Once the patient is feeling better, the patient can be discharged and switched to PO

diuretics and be followed up as an outpatient.

Lifestyle changes also prevent the development of heart failure: reducing obesity, smoking cessation, reducing alcohol consumption, decreasing sodium intake, controlling chronic conditions of hypertension, lipid disorders,
and diabetes.
