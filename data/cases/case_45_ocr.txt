Case Summary

Differential: Narcotic overdose, TCA overdose, Aspirin overdose, toxic alcohol ingestion, ethanol poisoning, acetaminophen overdose, other medication overdose.

This patient overdosed on opioids in an apparent suicide attempt. The patient had a recent life stressor (end of a relationship) and had access to narcotics that were either prescribed to her for her fibromyalgia or prescribed
to one of her family members or friends. Opioid intoxication is often a clinical diagnosis; naloxone should be empirically administered to any unresponsive patient, but this patient had several clues that pointed to this
toxidrome, specifically the pinpoint pupils (miosis) and decreased respiratory rate. Naloxone is a short-acting opioid antagonist that is preferably given IV but can be administered nasally, subcutaneously, or intramuscularly if
IV access cannot be obtained. Due to its short half-life and depending on the amount of opioids ingested or injected, patients can again become unresponsive or depress their respiratory rates after initial naloxone
administration, so it might need to be administered several times for sustained clinical improvement. Acute management of the unresponsive patient with suspected overdose includes obtaining a pulse oximetry, EKG and
cardiac monitoring, and IV access.

Although results of lab tests should not delay administration of naloxone, one should obtain basic labs (CBC, comprehensive metabolic panel, urinalysis, ABG) in addition to those specific for suspected overdose (urine tox
screen, acetaminophen level, salicylates, TCA level, lactate, ethanol level and toxic alcohol panel). The patient should be admitted to the inpatient setting, put on suicide precautions, and be seen by a psychiatry consult once
medically stabilized, who may decide to start her on psychiatric medications to help manage her mood symptoms. Activated charcoal should be administered when the patient is awake and responsive. Activated charcoal is
given in case the patient overdosed on any other substances or prescription medications which would prevent absorption of these things. There may be a concern that the patient overdosed on combination opioids (Le.
acetaminophen-hydrocodone), and the patient has not absorbed enough to cause symptoms or lab changes. Gastric lavage and NG tubes are not commonly used anymore in overdose. Activated charcoal is not given to
sedative patients via NG tubes because the patient is not protecting their own airway, and they can aspirate charcoal which can cause pneumonitis. Activated charcoal is best used if the patient ingested recently (<2 hours).
However, if it is an unknown ingestion at an unknown time, then giving activated charcoal is indicated (if the patient is alert/awake enough to swallow it as aforementioned). Psychiatry should be consulted, and the patient
should be put on suicide precautions and monitored for at least 24 hours before discharge to a psychiatric facility or to home depending on the results of the psychiatry consult.
