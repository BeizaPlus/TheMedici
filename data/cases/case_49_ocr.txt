Differential: Community acquired pneumonia, Lung cancer, Lung abscess, Bronchitis, Pulmonary embolus, Legionella pneumonia, COPD exacerbation, and Bronchiectasis.

This patient has community acquired pneumonia caused by a streptococcus pneumoniae infection. This should be high on the differential due to the patient's worsening productive cough, shortness of breath, and fever. After
doing a physical exam (which reveals lung findings consistent with a consolidation), you should obtain basic labs, including blood cultures and a sputum sample for culture and gram stain, as well as a chest X-ray. The next
step is to determine if the patient is going to be treated as an outpatient or an inpatient. The table given is specific to give an idea of what to look for. It is not important to memorize this, but to get the idea that if an older
patient has an abnormal lab value or medical condition, itis likely that the patient will need to come into the hospital to be treated. In this instance, the patient has concerning vital signs (fever, tachycardia, hypoxemia), a low
sodium at 129 and is 64 years old (so, 64-10 (due to being female) = 54 + 20 = 74), so the patient should be admitted to the hospital and started on appropriate antibiotics. The patient should be treated empirically before
the culture results come back. Typical inpatient regimens for community-acquired pneumonia (without concern for MRSA or pseudomonas) include beta-lactam + a macrolide OR respiratory fluoroquinolone monotherapy
(ie. levofloxacin). After treatment for approximately one day, the patient will begin to feel better, and the patient can be discharged, and the case will end. The patient can be given the recommended vaccines to prevent
future illnesses as well.
