The patient has secondary syphilis. From the history of present illness, the patient had a single painless chancre on his penis 6 weeks ago which resolved without treatment. This stage of a painless chancre at the site of
infection is called primary syphilis. Secondary syphilis which can last weeks to months. It can then go latent and become asymptomatic for years. It can then develop into tertiary syphilis which can involve multisystemic
systems including cardiovascular system, bones, neurosyphilis, and many other systems which can mimic many diseases. Syphilis is colloquially known as the great imitator.

The patient is currently experiencing common symptoms of secondary syphilis which can include: rash (disseminated or rash involving soles/palms as in this case), malaise, alopecia, fever, pharyngitis. Syphilis cases have
begun to increase again in the United States. It was previously on decline until 2000. Syphilis in men has been increasing primarily in the cohort of men who have sex with men. Methamphetamine use and frequent new sexual
encounters are risk factors for Syphilis.

HIV is a common coinfection for those with Syphilis, so this should be tested. Other sexually transmitted infections such chlamydia, gonorrhea, Hep A/B/C should be tested in this case.

Testing for Syphilis is done by performing both a nontreponemal test (RPR, VDRL, TRUST(not a current order on the USMLE)) and a treponemal test(FTA-ABS, MHA-TP, TPPA(not currently an order), TP-ElA(not a currently an
order), or ClA(not a currently an order)).

Typically, once a nontreponemal test such as a RPR or a VDRL is positive, then a confirmatory treponemal test will be done such as an FTA-ABS or an MHA-TP. The limitation of an FTA-ABS is that once it is positive, it will be
positive for life, so a previous infection could have made it positive. Although not done much anymore, a Darkfield Microscopy can be done which is definitive evidence of a Syphilis infection. For the USMLE to simplify, you
should simply get an RPR/VORL first, and if positive, get an FTA-ABS, MHA-TP, or a darkfield exam to confirm.

The patient should be treated with Penicillin G benzathine (2.4 million units IM x 1 dose). Other options in case the patient is allergic or had adverse reactions to penicillin would be: Doxycycline, Ceftriaxone, Tetracycline or
Amoxicillin. The patient's symptoms should resolve after treatment over the next 24-36 hours. The patient should be screened at 6- and 12-months following treatment to assure that the patient has become nonreactive to
syphilis screening tests.
