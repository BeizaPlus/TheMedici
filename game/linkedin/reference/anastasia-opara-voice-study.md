## Ana's Writing Voice — 12 Techniques

### 1. Opens with a single concrete example, not the thesis

From the Medium article:
> "So for example, let's say I want to build a 3D model of a bridge. In essence, what I have to do is convey that intent via a sequence of tool actions..."

She never starts with "the problem with content creation is..." She starts with "let's say I want to build a bridge." You can see the bridge. Then she pulls upward.

From the 80 Level interview:
> "Tiny Glade actually started as a hobby weekend project to test rendering, but I needed something in the scene. So I was like, 'That's cool, I can actually do procedural generation on the GPU'..."

The story is always: specific moment → realization → bigger idea.

### 2. Plain language for deeply technical things

From the Medium article:
> "every pixel asks 'if these are my neighbors, what is my color?'"

From the RustFest talk:
> "There is head scratching and more head banging on the wall, which is fun, exhausting, and sometimes outright terrifying, but never boring."

From the 80 Level interview:
> "Some of Tiny Glade's features have gone through five or six iterations, which is just crazy, but thanks to playtesting, we have now settled on something that works."

She explains rendering, compute shaders, and procedural algorithms the way you'd explain them to a smart friend over coffee. Zero jargon without translation. If a technical term appears, a plain-English version is right next to it.

### 3. Rhetorical questions as transition bridges

From the Medium article:
> "But hey! Does it mean we 'program' by giving an example? And the answer is yes."

From the Tiny Glade thread:
> "So, uh, HOW, right? 😅"

From the Medium article:
> "But what if we could automatically extract rules from examples?"

She uses questions to pull the reader forward. They feel conversational, not forced. Often answered immediately by herself — she's thinking out loud with you.

### 4. Enthusiasm that feels earned, not performative

From the Tiny Glade thread:
> "Aaaand... it barely got 20k views at the time 😅 Looking at it now, I'm not surprised. I really didn't get the TikTok format 🤦‍♀️"

From the undo-redo thread:
> "And of course I decided to make it animated. How hard can it be, right!? 😂"

From the 80 Level interview:
> "Oh my God, I was recognized. That's super cool."

She uses emoji freely, but never as filler. Each one carries an emotional beat — embarrassment, excitement, exhaustion, glee. The enthusiasm reads as genuine because she admits the hard parts too.

### 5. Humility + ambition in the same breath

From the Medium article:
> "Of course, getting to that workflow is a long journey. And even if we have ideas on how to get there, we don't have all the answers. And most likely, it won't be the answer, but a piece of an overall puzzle, where texture synthesis is a small step towards a bigger vision."

From the Tiny Glade thread:
> "I'm NOT a marketing expert or anything like that 😛 so I'm simply going to share what we did, to hopefully provide a useful data point for others."

From the 80 Level interview:
> "We set our goals: we want to learn the process, this is the first game among (hopefully) many, and recouping is a success."

She never overclaims. She says "maybe," "hopefully," "I think," "my theory is." But the vision underneath is massive. That combination — big ambition, small ego — makes you trust her.

### 6. Short sentences. Generous paragraph breaks.

From the Tiny Glade thread:
> "I promised I'd do at least five videos before giving up. I'm glad I did, because later ones went waaayy better: the third one got up to 3M views! 🤯 In retrospect, it was invaluable for testing how to talk about the game."

Breaks are frequent. Paragraphs are 1-3 sentences. It reads like breathing.

### 7. "We" over "I" — inclusive builder stance

From the Medium article:
> "At Embark, we believe it should be as easy to create as it is to play."

From the 80 Level interview:
> "We also needed a lot of compute shaders... Instead of prioritizing the limitations we have to work with, we could think about the target experience."

From the Tiny Glade thread:
> "We made our trailer fully in-house, taking all the lessons from what worked on TikTok and Twitter."

Even when she's the solo procedural artist on a feature, she says "we." It pulls the reader inside the team. You feel like you're part of the group figuring it out.

### 8. Admits confusion and struggle openly

From the RustFest talk:
> "There is head scratching and more head banging on the wall, which is fun, exhausting, and sometimes outright terrifying, but never boring."

From the undo-redo thread:
> "Undo for walls, however, turned into one of the most intricate bits of software engineering I've ever had to do 😅 A single eraser stroke can have many possible outcomes."

From the 80 Level interview:
> "I was waking up at 4 AM just so I could get something done before the actual work started at 9."

From the ivy thread:
> "Finding a suitable algorithm took me a few iterations 👀💦"

She never pretends it was easy. The difficulty is part of the story. It makes the result feel earned, and it makes her approachable.

### 9. Defines things by what they are NOT

From the Tiny Glade Steam description (cited in her thread):
> "no combat, management, or wrong answers — just kick back and make something pretty."

From the 80 Level interview:
> "Tiny Glade is not a very traditional game that is best described as more of a toy box for making dioramas."

From the Medium article:
> "the next step, in my opinion, is to go even further and ask how we can go directly from creative intent to content"

Negative definition is a powerful technique she uses repeatedly. Say what it's not first — then what it is lands harder.

### 10. Metaphors from real life, not from tech

From the 80 Level interview:
> "it's the same visceral pleasure as when the game recognizes dialogue options or remembers that you romanced this character"

> "akind to giving a child a bunch of LEGO bricks and waiting for their creativity to ignite"

From her viral marketing thread:
> "It was too good to be true! and an instant OMG YES from us 🥰"

From the RustFest talk:
> "the main art piece, and how computational drawing tries to capture that search"

Her metaphors come from games, film, childhood, emotion — never from computer science. She translates tech into human experience.

### 11. Handles objections before they're raised

From the 80 Level interview, on procedural vs AI:
> "For me, procedural art is very intimate because I create every single rule that goes into the system. In Tiny Glade, everything was hand-crafted by Tom and me, and therefore, we curate exactly the experience you will get out of it."

She knows readers might think "procedural = AI slop." She addresses it directly, with warmth, on her terms.

### 12. Closing is always an invitation, not a pitch

From the Medium article:
> "We'd love for all of you to try out example-based texture synthesis for yourselves."

From the RustFest talk:
> "if you have any questions, write a comment if you're watching it offline, or post a message in the chat if you're watching it live."

From the Tiny Glade thread:
> "Thank you for reading! 💚"

She never ends with "buy now" or "wishlist today." She ends with an open door. Try it. Ask me. Here's the code. The product sells itself; her job is just to show you the door.

## Ana's Speaking Voice (Interview Style) — Additional Notes

From the 80 Level interview — this is conversational Ana, not written Ana. Same voice, slightly looser:

**Tangible, unfiltered answers:**
> "We think that some players may even be surprised that they like it, while others may try it and be like, 'It was good, but it's not really my vibe.' And that's fair, but hopefully, it will be the former rather than the latter."

No corporate hedging. No "we believe our product delivers value." Just: some will like it, some won't, that's fair.

**Iterative process as narrative:**
> "One of the biggest issues we are continuously working on, because it is a thing that can't be figured out right away, is user experience."

> "And five brainstorming sessions later, we're just like, 'Doors — what does it mean?'"

She makes iteration feel like a story, not a project management update.

**Deep personal motivation, stated simply:**
> "I think I always wanted to make indie games, something personal and intimate but in the form of a video game made for others to enjoy and, hopefully, bring smiles to the players' faces."

No grandiosity. "Bring smiles" is the goal. It lands because it's small enough to be true.

### Trees & Flowers thread (Aug 19, 2022) — THE CANONICAL THREAD

This is the thread that best captures Ana's voice. Full verbatim, tweet by tweet.

**Tweet 1 — the anchor (joy first, details later):**
> "I've added trees 🌳 and flowers! 🌼 and there is a forest around! Suddenly it feels that much more real 🤩🥰 As usual, more details in the thread 🧵🤓"

**Tweet 2 — the rendering-as-painting metaphor:**
> "I feel that rendering is a lot like painting. You just need to... paint an illustration 60 times per second, and it has to work from different angles, and with different light directions 😅 And well, your brushes are code, but otherwise it's similar! Honest! 👀"

**Tweet 3 — credits the person she learned from:**
> "The canopy shader is based on @pomp_my_ride's fantastic tutorial 💚 Check it out. Thank you so much for sharing it, Pontus!"

**Tweet 4 — the one small addition she made:**
> "I have one small addition: a simple trick to hide obvious billboard intersections when the camera rotates... is to offset the depth in the pixel shader. Even a simple world-space noise hides the planar structure pretty well. Below is a video comparison 😊 Hopefully it's visible with twitter compression 🙈 (and big thanks to @h3r2tic for helping me with depth math 💚)"

**Tweet 5 — the surprise of simplicity:**
> "The peek-through-the-canopy part turned out to be way simpler than I had anticipated 😃 (a rare but very welcome bonus 😆) (Shoutout to @voxagonlabs for his depth of field shader 💙 — it hides all my artifacts so well 😂)"

**Tweet 6 — the algorithm, explained plainly:**
> "I discard pixels based on the dot product of their position in camera space and view direction. Then just add some noise, use textures to make it look more like leaves, et voilà 👩‍🍳😁 totally a canopy!"

**Tweet 7 — shows the ugly version (the thread's most important beat):**
> "I might be sharing nice-looking pictures (or I hope I am haha) but THIS is how it looked 80% of the time. I think a big part of 'gaining experience' is embracing this ugly duckling phase. I want to say 'becoming comfortable', but honestly it never stops being uncomfortable 🙈"

**Tweet 8 — the method (screenshots + notes):**
> "The more things you finish, the better you become at extrapolating your work-in-progress thing to a final result, and predicting the smallest step towards that. I took so many screenshots, and just noted what's wrong with them (see two huge columns). And step by step..."

**Tweet 9 — progression from chaos to character:**
> "... it went from a square of grass to a little meadow ✨ There are still missing elements, and it needs more polish, but it's starting to have a character of its own 🥰 PS: as usual thank you @h3r2tic, it wouldn't be where it is without your feedback 🙇‍♀️"

**Tweet 10 — the soft close:**
> "Thank you for reading all the way 💚 and stay tuned by following the newsletter 💌👐"

---

**Voice notes from this thread — beat by beat:**

1. **Joy anchors the thread.** No "I'm excited to share." Just "I've added trees and flowers! Suddenly it feels real." The exclamation marks are genuine, not marketing.

2. **The painting metaphor** — takes a deeply technical concept (real-time rendering at 60fps from all camera angles) and translates it into something anyone understands. Then the self-aware wink: "Honest! 👀" — she knows it sounds absurd. She's in on the joke with you.

3. **Credits her sources** — @pomp_my_ride, @h3r2tic, @voxagonlabs. Every technique has a name attached. She didn't invent it alone, and she tells you who helped. This builds trust: she's not pretending to be a lone genius.

4. **"One small addition"** — frames her contribution as modest. The depth-offset trick is clever, but she presents it as a "simple trick." Then immediately qualifies: "Hopefully it's visible with twitter compression 🙈" — the self-deprecation is the armor.

5. **"Way simpler than I had anticipated 😃 (a rare but very welcome bonus 😆)"** — admits when something went better than expected. This is as honest as admitting when something was hard. Both directions build credibility.

6. **"Et voilà 👩‍🍳😁 totally a canopy!"** — after a technical explanation (dot products, camera space, pixel shaders), she lands on the chef emoji and a playful "totally a canopy!" The technical and the playful live in the same sentence. That's the voice.

7. **SHOWS THE UGLY VERSION.** This is the thread's most important beat. "THIS is how it looked 80% of the time." She shows the garbage. Then the insight: "it never stops being uncomfortable 🙈" — not "I overcame it." The discomfort is permanent. You just learn to work through it. This is why devs trust her. She shows the part everyone hides.

8. **The method is visual, not theoretical** — "I took so many screenshots, and just noted what's wrong with them." Not "I employed an iterative feedback methodology." Screenshots. Notes. Two huge columns. Step by step.

9. **"Starting to have a character of its own 🥰"** — personifies the meadow. The work has a soul now. It's not "the system is approaching target fidelity." It's "the meadow has character."

10. **Closes with gratitude, not a pitch** — "Thank you for reading 💚" and a newsletter link that feels like an afterthought. No "WISHLIST NOW." No urgency. The work speaks.

### Why the Twitter voice worked

It is the voice of someone making something hard look fun. The threads follow a pattern: (1) show the thing (gif/video), (2) tell you it was hard in a funny way, (3) give you just enough technical detail to respect the craft, (4) close with warmth. The emoji aren't decoration — they're emotional compression. One 👀💦 replaces a paragraph about late nights and wrong approaches.

This is the voice that built 25K newsletter subscribers before the game even had a name. It's not marketing. It's just showing the work, honestly, with joy.

## Ana's Early Voice (2016–2017) — Before the Polish

These sources are Ana before she had an audience. Age ~21-22. The voice is more earnest, less playful than her later Twitter threads, but the DNA is the same. You can see the person who would become the Twitter Ana already forming.

### SideFX Tutorial "About Me" (Dec 2016) — The Earliest Self-Description

This appeared on SideFX's tutorial page for Lake Houses Vol. 1. She was still a Technical Artist Intern at EA Ghost Games. This is the seed everything grew from:

> Hello! My name is Anastasia Opara. Growing up in artists' family, I naturally developed a very strong interest in contemporary arts. By the age of 16, I have taken part in exhibitions (digital photography, drawings, short movies, poetry) in Russia, Germany, Czech Republic, Poland, Belgium, and Hungary; I was a participant of the annual international exhibition of contemporary art "OSRTALE'10" in Dresden, the I Moscow International Biennale of Young Art, and had my personal exhibition in the A3 Gallery in the center of Moscow. My unquenchable passion for arts led me to explore the field of 3D computer graphics, and in 2013 I joined NHTV International Game Architecture and Design in Netherlands with a speciality of 3D Visual Arts.

> During my education, the biggest revelation was the Introduction to Procedural Modelling course by Kim Goossens. The procedural approach completely blew me away. It seemed like mesmerising magic of art and programming, and my immediate reaction was "I want to be a wizard too!" Proceduralism became my absolute passion. Seeing things as a set of rules and patterns fascinates me, and every task becomes an absorbing puzzle when rendered through such a workflow.

> Currently, I'm a Technical Artist Intern at EA Ghost Games.

**Voice notes:**
- **"I want to be a wizard too!"** — The "magic" metaphor, born here. 21-year-old intern. Three years later she's writing about "programming by making art" on Medium. Six years later she's shipping Tiny Glade. The throughline is perfect.
- **"Unquenchable passion"** — This is pre-polish Ana. The word is slightly ornate / literary in a way she'd never use on Twitter. She's still finding her natural voice. But the substance — curiosity, persistence, refusal to separate art from code — is already fully formed.
- **"Mesmerising magic of art and programming"** — The two worlds collapsed into one. This is her entire career thesis in six words.
- **"Every task becomes an absorbing puzzle"** — This framing (work as puzzle, never grind) would later define the voice of her technical threads.
- She ends the bio at "intern at EA Ghost Games." Twelve years later, she'd sell 600K+ copies of a self-published indie game.

### "Realistic Procedural Architecture for Games" (Dec 2016) — First Major Interview

This is Ana's origin story, told in her own words. The interview covers her artistic childhood, her discovery of Houdini, and her philosophy of proceduralism. Key passages:

**On growing up in an artistic family (the most personal she's ever been publicly):**
> "I grew up in a quite unique artistic environment: since 4 years old, I was an active participant of an educational creative programme 'Artfor', created by my parents, contemporary media artists Vladimir and Maya Opara. When I was only 5, a Russian newspaper wrote about me as a youngest participant of the IV annual international exhibition-festival. By the age of 16, I have taken part in exhibitions in Russia, Germany, Czech Republic, Poland, Belgium, and Hungary."

> "Back then joining game industry never crossed my mind, until I discovered a game that was exhibited along with a more 'traditional' contemporary art media. It was such a novel and immersive experience that I instantly recognized that 3D Computer Graphics is the future."

**On discovering Houdini — the origin moment:**
> "I was introduced to Houdini during the 'Introduction to Procedural Modeling' course by Kim Goossens, whose enthusiasm and passion combined with the power and flexibility of Houdini absolutely blew me away. My love for experimentation thrived within the new workflow: being able to easily connect seemingly unrelated elements such as vertex position and sound was the kind of freedom that made me fall in love with Houdini. I felt like a child who was told she can learn how to do magic, and my immediate reaction was to go and acquire a wand."

**On proceduralism as dialogue:**
> "When working in Houdini, I feel like I have a dialogue, rather than a monologue, and that process is really addicting. The ability to track my thinking and capture it inside of a program by 'teaching' it to 'think' in the similar way I do mesmerizes me."

> "There were plenty of things I had to learn, however, I wouldn't necessarily label any of them as 'hard', but rather 'fun'. I guess, there is a fine line between those: when the challenge suits your level, it brings lots of joy and satisfaction, in the opposite case it can end in frustration."

**On constraints breeding creativity:**
> "I was allocating almost all of my free time that was rather limited having many other courses in the university. I had a mindset that any assignment is an opportunity to use Houdini and this approach showed me that a burning desire to learn will always find a way to bloom in any type of constraints."

**On the first serious project (Houdini Building Tool):**
> "It was a risky decision, as this type of workflow was new and there were no guarantees it's going to work, but my mind was set on achieving a positive outcome, thus filtering out any doubts and just hammering through all the challenges."

> "Since I wasn't familiar with vex or python back then, I built it with just basic nodes and simple h-script I knew, which already shows how much you can do with Houdini without really knowing programming."

**On the system surprising her:**
> "The most satisfying part was, when generating the final lake houses, the network would give me unexpected, but very creative results that I simply didn't anticipate. If that was a person, who built the house, I would praise him for originality! Does it mean that the systems can be creative within the constraints that we give them? I like to think of Houdini as a student, and you are a teacher."

**On proceduralism as painting — this metaphor appears here first:**
> "For me, this process resembles painting: first, you make very wide and general brush strokes and only then, when you are satisfied with that stage, you start refining and adding specifics."

**On pattern recognition:**
> "Seeing the world through the 'patterns and rules' glasses is pretty fascinating. For example, when I walk outside and see a piece of a rusty can, it mesmerizes me how many conditions have triggered to make this tiny piece be placed there."

**On beauty:**
> "We live in such a beautiful world and, as artists, we perform a unique role of 'beauty recording'."

---

**Voice notes from the 2016 interview:**

1. **"A child who was told she can learn how to do magic, and my immediate reaction was to go and acquire a wand."** — The most Ana sentence ever written. Metaphor from childhood. Pure wonder. Practical follow-through.

2. **"Dialogue, rather than a monologue"** — She frames the tool relationship as a conversation. This is the seed of her later "we" voice.

3. **"I wouldn't label any of them as 'hard', but rather 'fun'"** — Even at 21, she refuses the struggle narrative. She's specific about the line between productive challenge and frustration.

## How the Press Covers Ana vs. How Ana Covers Ana (The Undo-Redo Contrast)

The 80 Level article on Ana's undo-redo system (Jun 8, 2022, by Theodore McKenzie) is a useful contrast — not for Ana's voice, but for the *absence* of it.

**80 Level's framing (third-party journalism):**
> "This time, the developer tackled the undo-redo system for the walls, paths, and terrain. Much like the previous update, this new feature was made to make the usage of the generator more convenient and to have a simple way to fix things."

**Ana's own framing (the thread itself, May 28, 2022):**
> "This month I finally convinced myself to tackle the undo-redo system since it was becoming a premonition of a technical debt 👀 And of course I decided to make it animated. How hard can it be, right!? 😂"

**What the press strips out:**
- The personal ("*I* finally convinced myself")
- The emotional (the 👀 of dread, the 😂 of knowing it's going to hurt)
- The character ("a premonition of technical debt" — she personifies the debt like a ghost haunting her)
- The self-deprecating joke ("How hard can it be, right!?" — she knows. We know. That's the joke.)

**What the press adds:**
- Passive voice ("was made to make" — who made it? Ana. But the sentence doesn't care.)
- Redundancy ("more convenient and to have a simple way to fix things" — same thing said twice)
- Feature-list tone ("the developer added the ability to edit terrain to improve player experience")

**The lesson:** The press will always flatten your voice into a feature list. The only way to keep your voice alive is to tell the story yourself, in your own words, on your own channels — and do it before someone else does it for you. Ana's threads *are* the primary source. The 80 Level articles are just SEO wrappers that point back to them.

This is why the LinkedIn arc exists. It's not waiting for someone to write about Immersa. It's the primary source.
